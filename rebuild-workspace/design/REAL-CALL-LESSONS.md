# What four real recorded calls changed in the design

These lessons come from two calibration rounds against four real recorded calls, each read once, analysed
in a folder that was deleted with it, and not retained. Nothing identifying survives here: no
organisation, no person, no account, no client figures, no dates from any call, and tool references are
category words only.

The four calls span **three export grammars from more than one transcription tool, three seller
configurations and four meeting purposes**. The first pair — a group discovery call in which the client
shared their screen and walked their own build, and the tailored demo built from it — came from one export
tool, one seller pair and one account. The second pair broke that monoculture in every direction at once.
One is a consultant-led scoping session on an existing delivery client, where the client's own hands-on
operator shared a screen and walked a prototype they had built themselves, and where the deliverable is a
bespoke code artifact rather than configured structure. The other is a co-sell demo into a new account,
where a third-party representative fronted the meeting and the user drove the screen, in a room of nine-plus
people of whom three never spoke at all.

Round one's headline was that a real export is **dishonestly clean** — it marks no uncertainty and assigns
a confident speaker label to every fragment, including single letters. Round two confirms that and adds the
sharper finding: **the defect profile inverts between export families, and so does the correct behaviour by
call type.** Sentence shredding is universal. Everything else — merged multi-speaker blocks, very long
turns, split numbers, ellipses — belongs to one family and is structurally impossible in another. In the
same way, several rules written from the weak first pair would have graded the strongest calls in the
corpus as failures.

**125 changes.** 65 carry over from the first pair: **39 of those now hold independent confirmation** from a
second export tool, a second seller configuration or a second call type, and **50 were revised in this
round** (a number is often both). **60 are new**, numbered continuously from C66. Each row says what changes
and which skill, reference, script or fixture owns it. Paths are inside `plugins/monday-master-builder/`
unless noted; section numbers refer to the method spec in `dossier/research-delops-method.md`. A dagger (†)
marks a row revised in round two.

## What the second pair changed most

Six rules were wrong or too narrow after one pair of calls. Four of them would have misfired on the
*strongest* calls in the corpus — which is the most important lesson of this round: a rule induced from a
badly-run call punishes the behaviour that makes a well-run one work.

- **"Stop selling while they are showing" would have graded the best-run call in the corpus as a violation.**
  It was written for a client walking their current state, where our questions crowd out their narration.
  When a client instead walks a candidate solution they built themselves, sustained interrogation of that
  artefact *is* the work, and silence is the defect. The rule now carries its scope (C29 †).
- **The demo check-in rule would have deleted the two devices that made the best demo work.** "Every
  check-in to one named person, never to the room" was induced from a demo whose only check-ins were empty
  comprehension questions. In the strong demo a room-wide *open* check-in was the sole reason a silent
  attendee spoke at all — from a person whose stake our side did not know existed and therefore could not
  have named — and a closed comprehension check opened the floor to two substantive questions. The check-in
  is now two instruments with different jobs (C36 †).
- **Two more demo rules scored a good demo's best behaviour as failure.** The segment-count budget marks
  eighteen one-minute answers worse than eight long lectures, when the reverse is true; and "when no screen
  is tied to a pain, the client does the tying" reads client tying as a symptom, when it is what an
  interactive demo *produces*. The real defect is a client tie nobody confirms. Both rationales are
  replaced (C31 †, C37 †). And yet: every structural metric in that demo beat the weak one by two to five
  times and **the close was identical** — no commitment question, no per-pain confirmation, no date. The
  close is a separate discipline from delivery, and delivery discipline does not produce it.
- **The headline intake risk does not exist in one whole export family.** A design calibrated to split
  merged multi-speaker blocks arrives at a cue-bounded export, finds none, concludes the file is clean —
  and quotes dozens of cross-speaker fragments wrongly. The two defects are anti-correlated: one family
  buys clean turn boundaries by merging, the other buys clean blocks by shredding. Intake must run both
  detectors on every file and expect only one to fire (C5 †, C10 †, C70).
- **The citation contract forbade quoting almost anything on a cue-bounded export.** "Never let a quote
  cross a turn boundary" was written where a turn is a paragraph. Where a unit is a breath, a single
  sentence routinely spans three or four units of the *same* speaker and roughly two fifths of adjacent
  units share one label. The clause doing the safety work is the *speaker* boundary, and that is what it
  now says (C11 †).
- **The claims rules were written for generated text, and every damaging claim in the second pair was
  improvised speech.** Two prices for the same purchase, minutes apart and several-fold apart, were spoken
  aloud; the entitlement and scale answers were guessed live; and the single worst exposure in the corpus
  was our own live operational board on screen, with staff, hours and rate pointers on it, which no lint
  can see because it never enters a document. The discipline now reaches speech and screens (C45 †, C50 †,
  C123).

## 1. Intake and parsing

**The finding (round one).** In both files the three most load-bearing quotes — the headline effort figure,
the pivotal objection, and the richest adoption evidence — are all mis-attributed. One labelled block of
roughly a thousand words, under a single first name, contained at least six speaker changes including the
seller's own questions and a passage addressing by name the very person the block was attributed to.
Sentence shredding was pervasive: one sentence routinely spans two to four turns and two or three labels,
so the opening fragment is credited to whoever spoke before. The headline figure never appears as a whole
token anywhere in either file. Intake tuned on the fixtures reads all of this as high-confidence.

**The finding (round two).** Two more exports, two more grammars, and the defect profile **inverts**. The
cue-bounded export emits no merged block that is even structurally possible — its longest unit is under a
hundred words — and instead wedges one-word fragments of one speaker inside another's run, with the
boundary gap at a millisecond. The other export has zero merged blocks too, and instead shreds so hard that
roughly one turn in five carries words owned by somebody else, and roughly two thirds of its one-word turns
belong to a different speaker than the label says. Shredding, dishonest cleanliness, identity collapse,
invisible screen content, corrupted ordinary-word names and damaged numbers are **universal**. Merged
blocks, very long turns, ellipses, split thousands and colliding timestamps are **family-specific** and one
family cannot produce them. So is every threshold: the four-word constant is mis-sized in both directions
on the second pair, and the shred rate varies nearly fourfold inside one vendor's own family.

## 2. Discovery grading

**The finding (round one).** The discovery call was excellent — five excavated pains, a complete
current-state map, a stated leadership win condition — and the rubric returned **Not ready** on one
criterion, because no cost of the current state was ever confirmed. Meanwhile four other criteria could
plausibly have been graded Met from the *seller's* own sentences: a renewal date the seller named and no
client ever acknowledged, a closing recap followed by an agreement that actually answered a meeting
proposal, a capability list nobody objected to, and backchannel tokens that the notetaker had put on the
wrong side. Three of those misreads would flip the verdict. The rubric also assumes one or two client
voices; this room had nine.

**The finding (round two).** The cost criterion **is** satisfiable, and the first pair made it look
impossible. On the scoping call a client scoped an effort figure to the pain in a single clause,
unprompted, with no cost question asked at all — because the person who performs the work was in the room
and the meeting was about making their work smaller. The mechanism generalises: a bleed is obtained in
proportion to how narrow and countable the unit of work is, and on a consultant-led working session it
arrives as a by-product of the scoping argument rather than as an answer to a cost question. That verdict
came out **Nearly ready**. What broke instead was different: the rubric cannot express delegation with a
reserved approval gate, cannot record what the client has already built, cannot distinguish "we never
asked" from "the right role was not in the room", and has no home for the objection that changing what we
previously built costs them.

| # | Change | Where |
|---|---|---|
| C14 † | Add an explicit **non-evidence list**: our own framing and recaps, backchannel tokens, agreement that answers a meeting proposal rather than a substantive claim, feature requests phrased as questions, and any date **or figure** we supplied. ## 3. Demo scripts

**The finding (round one).** The real demo inverted the method: presenter talk share in the low eighties
(higher still during the screen share), two single turns of roughly 1,400 and 1,600 words, three check-ins
across fifty-one minutes of showing, none of the eight showings inside the two-minute cap, zero per-segment
callbacks, zero exit ramps taken from three buying signals, and no commitment question at the close. The
most-cited pain from discovery never got a screen; the most-repeated ask was raised five times by four
people across both calls and answered in words every time. Every screen presupposed data the client does
not have, which discovery had recorded a month earlier. And the person driving the screen left nine
minutes before the end, mid-thread, so the close became a drift — during which the strongest buying signal
of the hour arrived with nothing on screen.

**The finding (round two).** The second demo is the control case, and it beats the first on every structural
metric by two to five times: client share around a third of the call, longest presenter turn under two and
a half minutes, eighteen check-ins and stops rather than three, sixteen of eighteen showings inside the
cap, both presenters present through the end, and mid-demo discovery that produced the call's best pain
statement. **The close was identical** — no commitment question, no per-pain confirmation, no date, and a
nonverbal read of the room standing in for all three, in a room where three people had not spoken at all.
Three consequences. The close is a separate discipline that delivery quality does not deliver. Two
presenters produce a defect class one cannot: the monologue becomes a duet, and every per-speaker
instrument passes it while the two longest client-free stretches run over four minutes each. And three of
the round-one rules would have marked this demo's best moments as failures.

## 4. Vocabulary and structure mapping

**The finding (round one).** Reusing the client's own labels was the single most method-compliant thing in
the demo, and it worked — their own stage names came straight off their screen and nobody had to ask what
a screen meant. It was still not enough. Their structural model and the demo's structural model were
different, and nobody said so out loud, so the client did the mapping silently and concluded the demo
solved the management layer rather than the job layer. That verdict was the consequence of a missing
two-minute translation beat, not of a missing feature.

**The finding (round two).** The same failure reproduced exactly in a different vertical, with a different
presenter and a different export tool, and this time two client speakers surfaced it themselves within
ninety seconds — one describing why they had moved away from the demoed shape, another describing having
built that shape and being unable to use it, and a third party in the room declining to choose between
them. That is not a conflict to resolve by recency; it is a **fork**, and the design has no object that can
hold one. It also has no way to record that a client's system of record is authoritative for some fields
and a follower for others, which is the ordinary case rather than the exception.

## 5. Claims discipline

**The finding (round one).** Fifteen product claims in the demo were checked against our own ninety live
probes: three are contradicted by what we measured, eight were unverified yet stated as certainties, one is
outside our testing entirely (a vendor's data-handling and training policy), and three breach the method's
own rules regardless of truth (a usage statistic and two other-client anecdotes). The client's most
specific question of the call drew the least reliable answer in it — an unqualified "absolutely" to a
cascade-edit question that three of our probes say is exactly where silent wrongness lives.

**The finding (round two).** Fifty-three claims across two more calls, and the problem has moved. These
rules were written for **generated scripts**, and nine of one call's twelve claims and all of another's cost
figures were **improvised answers to live questions**, which no script check can reach. Consumption pricing
is the corpus's biggest hole: six money figures from our own side inside one meeting, internally
inconsistent by several-fold, against a dossier that records no such pricing at all — and the person who
had claimed the approval decision had already left the call when the corrected figure was spoken to a
colleague with instructions to relay it. Two of our own people gave opposite answers about how the same
metered allowance behaves. And the most serious exposure of the round never entered a document at all: our
own live operational board, with engagement names, hours, billable flags and an explicit pointer to where
staff rates sit, on screen to a prospect whose room included a second company.

## 7. Call type, seat and routing

**The finding.** Call type is not one label but **four dimensions**, and each one demonstrably switches
which criteria apply: *seat* (who owns the room and the question order — partner-led, co-sell, vendor-led,
client-led), *relationship* (new logo, existing client of ours, a third party's account where we are the
stranger), *direction of show* (we show, they show their current state, they show a candidate solution they
built, nobody shows), and *output class* (configured structure, bespoke code, hybrid). Two modifiers change
thresholds without changing structure: room shape, and whether money is live in the room. Across four calls

## 8. Scoping and bespoke-build engagements

**The finding.** One call in four had a deliverable that is not platform structure at all, and the product
has no artifact type for it, no routing for it, and no way to hold what it produces. The client's own
internal builder had prototyped most of the solution alone, hit a wall, named the wall precisely, and asked
for help finishing it — which the existing objection vocabulary files as a build-it-ourselves risk when it
is the strongest buying signal in the corpus. Everything the design assumes about a build breaks: there is
no structure to read back, no automation list to approve, no navigable build file, the probe list is
irrelevant, the audit is a code review, and **the thing cannot be demonstrated before it exists**, so the
demo-script machinery has no input. A scoping call is accepted at the door today and routed nowhere: the
term for this kind of meeting appears nowhere in the architecture, and the post-intake chain covers intro,
discovery, demo and sign-off calls only. Alongside that sit three things with no home at all — an
unresolved structural fork, an external dependency, and a pre-sale demo sequence that predates any version.

## 9. The voice specification

**The finding.** A voice profile built by pooling "our side" across a corpus puts other people's habits in
the user's mouth. Measured across two calls, one colleague's hedging tic runs at roughly twenty-five times
the user's rate per thousand words, and the closing tic the design already bans is four-for-four on the
other side of the room and zero-for-zero on the user's. One label-level attribution of that tic is even on
the wrong side until shred repair moves it. The user's own register is measurable and reproducible —
assertive rather than hedged, mechanism-first, with hedging done explicitly and structurally ("I would want
to check that") rather than atmospherically — and it contains a complete repertoire for not knowing, not
being able, and self-correcting that the method has almost no vocabulary for.

| # | Change | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **C121** | **Voice profiles are per-speaker and per-seat. Never pool "our side" across a corpus.** Exclude any turn adjacent to a same-side turn whose content could belong to either speaker; a voice bank is built only from turns with unambiguous side-internal attribution. The corpus contains a clean specimen of why: one recommendation that decided an engagement's tooling is labelled to the person who asked the question it answers, and either reading is coherent | new `shared/house-style.md`, `call-intake` |
| **C122** | **Ship a voice specification the script generator follows**, fully genericised: measured register targets, a DO list, a DON'T list, and a bank of sentence *shapes* with placeholders. The DOs that carry weight: open with who you are, who is with you and why, and what you already know from the pre-read — proof of preparation beats a capability list; define the platform once in a sentence with the jargon dissolved on the spot whenever anyone present has not seen it; anchor every new construct to whatever they already use; prefer building live on screen to describing; stop before a dependency and ask rather than assuming a fact about the client; route every question to a named person **with the reason they own it**; ask the same architecture question twice, once of whoever knows what is possible and once of whoever knows what is needed; mark the load-bearing primitive and confirm it before composing on it; convert a client statement into a named mechanism, define the name immediately in plain words, and restate it in their world; close each proposed mechanism with a correctability question, which invites the missing case rather than an opinion; admit unfamiliarity and make the admission the reason for the question; narrate fumbles and say why a screen is messy; retract explicitly and attribute the correction; deflate scope when it is genuinely small; anchor money against a spend they already make and check entitlement before quoting; ask for their example artefact before offering ours. The DON'Ts are the delivery-side statement of C45, C46, C49, C50, C51, C97, C100, C123 and C124 in the second person, plus: never run past a couple of minutes without a routed question, and never let a client have to apologise for interrupting. **Scope caveat:** the bank rests on a large sample in one seat and a much smaller one in another, so the framing, mechanism and commercial shapes drawn from the smaller sample are provisional and are regenerated when a second call in that seat arrives | new `shared/house-style.md`, `demo-script`, client-facing reply outputs |

## 10. Confidentiality exposure during delivery

**The finding.** The leak lint reads payloads and files. It cannot see a screen, and the worst exposure in
the corpus never entered a document: our own live operational board, opened to a prospect whose room
included a second company, showing engagement names as item names, who worked what hours, billable flags,
our automation architecture, and an explicit spoken pointer to where staff rates sit one click further. The
presenter narrated the sensitivity while displaying it — and had stopped a share two and a half minutes
earlier precisely to avoid exposing something. Nothing in the existing set reaches this: the nearest rule
governs an assistant-to-platform connection, not our own account as demo material. The same section covers
two adjacent exposures: a senior colleague floating a client's personal login as an access option, which
the client immediately upgraded to a proper named invitation unprompted; and asking a client to open
something on their own shared screen, which produced a near miss the client handled themselves.

| # | Change | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **C123** | **Never demonstrate from our own live operational account** — the one carrying staff, rates, client commercials or engagement records. Internal examples come from a purpose-built demo environment with seeded data, and the product refuses to generate a run-sheet step that shows our own account. The prep block names the demo account, asserts it is clean of rates, margins, staff cost and client lists before the call, and carries an explicit **do-not-open list headed by our own commercial boards**. **Pair the ban with the prep item that removes the incentive**: the breach here was the cost of an unprepared answer to a foreseeable capability question — permissions, in this case — because the demo environment had no example of it. This is the only change in the round that removes a cause rather than policing a symptom, and it is adopted on **severity rather than frequency**: one observation, but the harm is realised, irreversible and third-party. Narrow it to the live operational account, so showing a reference or template account of ours when a client asks to see something real remains available | `segments.md` prep block, `shared/safety.md` |
| **C124** | **Never request a client's credentials, and generate exactly one access-request shape.** A named seat at our own domain, in the name of the person doing the work, at the lowest level that can perform it, with the seat type and its reach stated; where elevated rights are genuinely required, that is a separate request carrying its reason and its duration. Never mention a client's personal login — **including as a hypothetical, and including while naming the risk**, because naming it is an invitation: on the one observation, floating it produced an immediate offer of a broader grant than the work needed. Phrase the rule as binding on **everyone on our side**, not on the user alone; the request came from a colleague. The harm is established from our own dossier rather than from the call: every connector action is logged as the authorising identity, and an over-privileged seat is a standing exposure on every engagement | `shared/safety.md`, `account-build` |
| **C125** | **Screen-share etiquette when the client is driving: ask for the shape, not the contents.** Ask what form something takes — whether an address ends in an identifier or is a preview link — rather than asking them to open a specific item we chose; request one sanitised example out of band instead; and if something unintended appears, name it and move on. **Do not over-reach into a ban on asking them to reveal anything**, which would forbid the structure-inventory probing C22 mandates and which in the observed case was a legitimate question that shaped the whole build. *Provisional (n=1), and no harm occurred — the client self-corrected in one turn. A call where such a request actually exposes something would promote it* | `shared/safety.md`, `segments.md` |

## Decisions these lessons still need

All eleven decisions carried from round one are now **answered**, and none is left open — the second pair
was chosen to settle exactly the ones a single export tool and a single seller could not. Each answer names
the evidence that settles it; where a decision is answered by a **measurement across export families**,
that is said, because the affordability of the conservative answer usually depends on it. Eight new
decisions take their place, and they are harder: they are about scope and ownership rather than about
reading a transcript.

1. **Cross-speaker pain composition — ANSWERED: yes, permissive, with a guard.** The best-evidenced pain in
   the corpus is composed across three client speakers in the same process — one states it, one prices the
   effort, one quantifies the workaround's failure — eleven minutes apart, with none of them corroborating
   another. Under the strict reading it is not excavated. Guard: same process, and speaker-per-check
   recorded (C19).
2. **"For how long" — ANSWERED: both, as two independently graded fields.** One call gives process time and
   no upkeep time at all; another gives upkeep time qualitatively and no process time. Neither call can
   answer the other's reading, so the criterion is Met on either and the record shows which (C28 †).
3. **Evidence inside an unattributable passage — ANSWERED: Partial on side-only evidence, never Missing.**
   This is affordable precisely because the problem is family-specific: a whole export family produces no
   composite blocks at all, so the conservative answer does not fire on every file.
4. **Unpriced bleed frame — ANSWERED: acceptable, and now with a third state.** On the scoping call volume,
   frequency and an effort rate all existed and only a multiplier was missing, which the unpriced frame
   could not express; the frame stays, the "not measurable from their record" state stays, and
   effort-quantified/rate-pending joins them (C18 †).
5. **Next step: a date, or "agreed, date to follow"? — ANSWERED: neither, exactly.** Across four calls
   **none fixed a new date on the call**, and the only date in the corpus pre-existed and was named by our
   side. The differentiator between a good close and a bad one is a **mechanism** with a named owner, not a
   date (C26 †).
6. **Client-confirmed from a turn that swallowed the client's assent — ANSWERED: no, permanently
   unprovable, and it recurs.** A third and fourth independent instance appeared in a different export
   grammar, one of them the only place in an hour where a callback drew an explicit confirmation. Treat as
   non-evidence, and add a prep consequence: re-ask it in the next call, naming the person.
7. **Heuristic split of composite blocks — ANSWERED: keep whole-and-flagged, and the cost is lower than
   feared.** The strict answer only bites on one export family, so it is affordable. Split into candidate
   segments marked unresolved for reading; never let a quote out of one.
8. **ASR-corrupted names — ANSWERED: store corrupted, resolve alongside.** The decisive case is that the
   user's own firm name appears in several renderings across the corpus, at least one of them inside their
   own opening self-introduction, and the platform's own name is mangled in the same sentence. A repair
   pass would have silently rewritten a self-introduction.
9. **Are seller-led calls first-class? — ANSWERED: yes, and the question was cut in the wrong place.** The
   axis that matters is not who is selling but **seat**, and there are three of them, not two. They differ
   in what we may do, not merely in who talks: in a seat we do not own, the next-step and scope criteria are
   graded against the *thread* rather than against us. Answered by C105 and C108.
10. **A source of truth for plan limits and quotas — ANSWERED: yes, urgently, and it must reach live
    speech.** A dated, user-approved claims store kept **outside the client folder**, plus a read-live-at-prep
    step, plus the rule that an unsourced figure is refused rather than ranged. The harm is measured: a
    several-fold in-call price revision, and two further unsourced entitlement claims in another call
    (C45 †, C50 †, C102).
11. **Tool-specific defect profile? — ANSWERED, and it splits cleanly.** **Universal:** sentence shred,
    one-word fragments attributed across the side boundary, lowercase-initial turns, misattributed
    backchannel, untrustworthy punctuation, corrupted ordinary-word names, damaged numbers, zero
    uncertainty markers. **Family-specific:** merged multi-speaker blocks and very long turns occur only in
    paragraph-merging exports and are *structurally impossible* in cue-bounded ones, where the mirror-image
    defect appears instead — utterances truncated at unit boundaries and long-monologue detection destroyed
    by the unit cap. Every *constant* is export-specific and varies several-fold inside one vendor's own
    family. Do not hard-code the turn grammar or any threshold: measure the file, then set thresholds from
    the measurement (C70, C71).

### New decisions this round raises

12. **Is bespoke, non-platform work in scope at all, or only recorded and handed off?** The scoping call's
    deliverable is an application. Either the product grows a scope artifact and non-platform element kinds
    and *designs* such work, or it says plainly that such strands are recorded and handed off. C115
    ships the recording half only; the designing half needs an explicit yes.
13. **One record per client, or one per engagement?** A prior build of ours that is still live argues for
    one record per account with strands of work inside it. That changes the version model, the build lease,
    the audit and close-out, all of which are per-record today, and two concurrent strands would contend
    for one lease.
14. **Does a blocking fork refuse the approval freeze?** It is the safe answer and it adds a new way for
    the product to say no. Who sets the blocking flag must be the user's call; whether the product may
    refuse a freeze on it needs an explicit yes.
15. **Where does metered consumption sit relative to the bleed frame?** C103 argues consumption is a design
    input; C18 already builds an unpriced bleed frame. Neither the analysis nor the verification could
    settle whether consumption belongs inside that frame or in a section of its own, and a budget holder
    will ask about both in one breath.
16. **Which wins when the conservative fallbacks collide?** An unresolved output class makes every
    capability unverified; an unresolved type demotes every gate to advisory. Together the product can
    reach a state where it will neither assert nor gate. Pick the winner explicitly (C105).
17. **Does the product model the demo environment as an asset?** If not, every pre-sale demo sits outside
    it — which on this corpus is most of the work — and the live-change inventory has nowhere to live
    (C88, C90).
18. **What credential substitutes for the banned client-count claim in a seat with no third party to
    vouch?** C49's substitute is a verifiable credential, and the observed instance was one a third party
    supplied in the room. In a partner-led seat there is nobody to supply it, which is exactly where the
    credentialing need is highest.
19. **The corpus still has a seller monoculture.** Rounds one and two together cover four calls, three
    export tools and three seats — but every behavioural rule in the claims and delivery sections describes
    the habits of a handful of people across two firms. The export-tool monoculture is fixed; the seller
    monoculture is not. Roughly twenty of the new changes rest on a single observation and are marked
    provisional, each with the second observation that would confirm it. Treat them as hypotheses until it
    arrives.
